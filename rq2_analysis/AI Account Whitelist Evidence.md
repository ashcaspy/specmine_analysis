# AI Account Whitelist: Evidence and Limitations (RQ2)

## 1. Purpose

This document explains how we selected the AI-associated accounts used in our RQ2 analysis, the evidence supporting their inclusion in the whitelist, and the limitations associated with using repository information to determine authorship. The 18 accounts included in `ai_account_whitelist_evidence.csv` were taken directly from the `HIGH_CONF_AI` list in our `SpecMine RQ2.ipynb` notebook, and no accounts were added, removed, or reclassified when preparing these files. These documents were created to provide additional information about the methodology already used in our analysis rather than introducing a new classification procedure or generating additional experimental results, and therefore they are maintained as supporting documentation instead of being regenerated each time the notebook is executed.

## 2. Account Selection and Classification

To address RQ2, we used commit information from the SpecMine `spec_file_commits` dataset to examine the recorded origins of specification artifacts, focusing on fields such as `author_login`, `committer_login`, `author_type`, `message`, and `file_url_sha16`, which provide information about the accounts associated with changes to each specification. We initially examined broader approaches for identifying AI-associated activity, including AI-related keywords in commit messages and account names, but these approaches were not used for the final classification because references to AI tools do not necessarily indicate that the specification itself was generated by AI, and similarly, the presence of an automated account does not automatically establish that the account represents an AI coding agent. As a result, we used a more conservative whitelist consisting of 18 accounts with names associated with recognizable AI tools or agents, which allowed us to limit the classification to specific account identifiers rather than relying on broad keyword patterns that could incorrectly identify unrelated activity.

The classification procedure checks whether either the author or committer login matches an account included in the whitelist, using case-insensitive matching and requiring the complete account name to match rather than searching for partial keywords. Some of the accounts contain the `[bot]` suffix, which provides an indication that the account may be automated, although this does not independently confirm that an AI system generated the specification, and accounts without this suffix introduce additional uncertainty because their identities cannot be determined from their usernames alone.

## 3. Authorship Categories

After identifying the relevant commit information, the results were grouped by the specification identifier `file_url_sha16`, allowing the available author and committer information to be considered when assigning each specification to an authorship-origin category.

The categories used in the analysis are:

- **AI-associated:** Specifications with commit information matching an account included in the AI whitelist.
- **Human-associated:** Specifications with commit information identifying a GitHub user who is not included in the AI whitelist.
- **Collaborative:** Specifications containing both AI-associated and human-associated commit signals.
- **Unknown:** Specifications where the available commit information is insufficient to establish one of the other categories.

These classifications are based on the origin information recorded in the repository rather than an independent evaluation of the specification content, and while they provide a consistent way to compare groups of artifacts, they do not necessarily identify who actually wrote the specification. For example, a developer may use an AI coding tool to generate or modify specification content and then commit those changes through a personal GitHub account, which could result in the specification being classified as human-associated even though AI contributed to its development, while a commit associated with an AI-related account does not establish that the entire specification was generated by AI. For this reason, the categories are interpreted as observed commit-origin classifications throughout the analysis rather than definitive human or AI text-authorship labels, which is particularly important when considering the differences identified between groups in RQ2.

## 4. Supporting Evidence and Limitations

The accompanying `ai_account_whitelist_evidence.csv` file contains the 18 accounts used in the classification procedure, along with the observable evidence associated with each account and its independent verification status, which allows the exact whitelist used in the analysis to be reviewed alongside the methodology. The evidence supporting the whitelist primarily consists of recognizable account names associated with AI tools or agents and, where applicable, the presence of the `[bot]` suffix, and while these characteristics provide a more specific basis for classification than general keyword searches, they do not independently establish account ownership or confirm that an AI system was responsible for producing the specification content. We were unable to independently verify the identities of all 18 accounts using official provider documentation or repository ownership records, and therefore the classifications remain subject to uncertainty even though the whitelist was selected conservatively.

One limitation of this approach is that an account could be incorrectly classified as AI-associated if its username resembles an AI service without actually being connected to that service, while specifications developed with AI assistance may also be classified as human-associated when the changes are committed through a developer's personal account. Additionally, AI tools or automated accounts that are not included in the whitelist may not be identified, and the commit information does not provide enough evidence to determine the extent of human or AI involvement in producing the final specification. The variable name `HIGH_CONF_AI` reflects our decision to use a smaller and more conservative set of AI-associated account identifiers rather than broader matching criteria, but it should not be interpreted as a verified probability of AI authorship or as confirmation that every account included in the list belongs to an AI system. These limitations are considered when interpreting the results of the authorship comparisons, as the observed differences between groups reflect the available repository evidence rather than independently verified authorship of the specification text.

4. Examine how the commit information is grouped by `file_url_sha16` and how the available AI-associated and human-associated signals are used to assign the resulting categories
5. Interpret the RQ2 comparisons using these observed commit-origin categories while considering the limitations associated with identifying actual text authorship from repository metadata

Because the whitelist is explicitly defined in the notebook and the same account identifiers are documented in the accompanying CSV, the classification procedure can be reproduced using the available SpecMine commit information without requiring a separate AI-authorship detection model.
